Creative Team Brief · Story & Diagnosis · Master

The Vampire Prince

The whole story, told for a first-time reader — and why the strongest holder in the batch still loses its audience

Full show: 665 episodes (~82.5h) · story from a full read of the summaries · retention read hour-for-hour vs The Alpha's Bride (TAB) · audience signal from ~16,700 in-app comments

A human noblewoman is married off to a vampire prince to buy a peace — and finds the monster is the gentlest thing in the kingdom. For two hundred episodes this show out-performs the benchmark by a distance no other title in the batch approaches. What costs it its audience is not a bad plot turn. It is a thirty-episode flashback, a second name for the hero, and a paywall.

Who's who
Lady Evielyn “Evie” of Ylvia
A human noblewoman married off to the vampire prince as a political peace offering. She drives this show — and keeps driving it to the last episode.
Prince Gavriel
The vampire prince who takes her as his bride and turns out to be the gentlest thing in the story. Later revealed to carry a second, older self.
Gavrael
Gavriel's buried past-life self, whose childhood with Evielyn is told in a thirty-episode flashback. Note how close the two names are — that is the problem.
Vera & Gideon
A secondary couple who carry a long middle stretch. Unusually, the audience stayed with them.
Caius, Thea & Lorcan
The crown prince who warns her, the woman at court who unsettles her, and the enemy building an alliance against the vampires.
Onyx, Beatrice & Belial
Her dragon; her mother; the king — the court and war cast that fills the middle.
The problem, in one line
This is the strongest first half in the batch — blocks as low as 0.21× the benchmark's drop rate, still holding 60% of its audience at ep 210. The first real bleed arrives at ep 231–290, when the present-day story stops for a thirty-episode flashback and the hero splits into two names one letter apart (Gavriel / Gavrael); it peaks at 2.11× around ep 291–310, where the comments are asking who the characters are. The show then recovers for a hundred and sixty episodes. The apparent catastrophe at the end — a 6.75× block — is the free-unlock window closing around ep 565, not a story failure. Over H10→H30, of 100 listeners 61 stay against the benchmark's 67.
Two data corrections made before this read. (1) Episode 270's LDAU is recorded at 15,019 against neighbours of ~7,500 — an exact double-count, interpolated out of the level curve; the drop-rate column at that episode is normal, so the block ratios were unaffected. (2) The pack's notes list live length as equal to running length, but the comments from ep 560 onward state plainly that free episodes stopped and the last stretch had to be bought. The comments are decisive and this brief treats ep ~565 as a paywall boundary.
Part I · The story, in full
How the story progresses
How to read this section. The left column is the story only — what happens, told straight. The panel on the right is the diagnosis — what the audience did there, on the numbers, plus their own words. Kept apart on purpose.
Act 1

The Midnight Bride

Episodes 1–30
What happens in the story

Lady Evielyn of the House of Ylvia dresses for a midnight wedding she never wanted. She had once imagined a sunny aisle and a groom of her choosing; instead she is the daughter handed over to keep a peace. Her mother holds her and reminds her why she has to go through with it.

Her husband is Prince Gavriel, and the vampire court she has been warned about does not behave as promised. In the carriage he asks if she is frightened and dislikes how she flinches when he speaks; he tells her that no one is allowed to hurt her, not even him. When she says she thought vampires were cold, he answers that he is the only exception.

At his castle she finds human maids, Fray and Gina, and is fed steak at his own table. He clears the red from his eyes when it frightens her and wraps her when she goes cold. At the welcome ball she assumes the courtesy is a trap; Crown Prince Caius warns her that Gavriel is not as harmless as he looks, and something tightens in her chest when another woman touches his face.

The audience here
The best opening hold in the batch

From ep 51 the show sheds only 0.4–0.5× the benchmark's rate — it is not merely holding, it is out-performing a hit by a wide margin. A frightened bride, a courteous monster, and a court full of knives.

Act 2

The Vampire Court

Episodes 31–120
What happens in the story

The marriage warms. A first kiss becomes a second until he forces himself to stop and sends her to bed; the household begins to treat her as their lady rather than a hostage. She learns the politics from the maids — why her husband is on bad terms with the emperor.

Around the romance a war assembles. Gavriel fights, and fights brutally; the emperor manoeuvres; Lorcan builds an alliance with the very humans who despise vampires. When Gavriel loses his composure over the question of siring an heir, the personal and the political collide.

Evie stops being cargo. By the time she stands in the rain, straightened to her full height and refusing to be a damsel, she is a participant in her own story.

The audience here
Still far under benchmark pace

Blocks run 0.3–0.9×. Almost three-quarters of the ep-51 audience is still listening at ep 130. The forced-marriage-to-devotion engine plus a gathering war is doing all the work, and doing it exceptionally well.

Act 3

Dragons and the War

Episodes 121–230
What happens in the story

The scale grows: an emperor threatened by an unseen voice, orc raids, and then a dragon emerging from the clouds. Evie names it Onyx, and more follow.

This is the show's most confident stretch. The war escalates, the dragons arrive, and Evie is at the centre of both — not protected from the plot but inside it.

The audience here
Exceptional — the strongest run in the batch

Blocks fall as low as 0.21× the benchmark. Sixty percent of the opening audience is still there at ep 210. Whatever else this show does later, the first two hundred episodes are a model of how to hold an audience.

Act 4

Gavrael — the Thirty-Episode Flashback

Episodes 231–290
What happens in the story

The present-day story stops. In its place the show runs thirty consecutive episodes titled “Gavrael (Part I)” through “Part XXVIII” — a flashback to a hidden sixth empire beneath the Middle Land, and to the childhood of a boy called Gavrael and a girl called Evielyn.

It is a real revelation: Gavriel carries an older, buried self, and there is a darkness in him that he cannot bring back to the surface world. When the present resumes he knows he must leave her.

But for a month of listening the audience is in the past, with a character whose name differs from the hero's by one letter.

The audience here
The first real bleed — and it is a confusion problem

Blocks move from under-benchmark to 1.5–1.7× and stay there for sixty episodes. Two things are happening at once: the present-day romance stops, and the male lead splits into two nearly identical names. The comments are not bored — they cannot tell who they are listening to.

Act 5

Two Names, One Man

Episodes 291–330
What happens in the story

Back in the present, Gavriel takes Evie to a lake, and a court of light faes kneels to her and pledges to her war. The pieces are being set for the real conflict, and Evie is gathering allies of her own.

The confusion, however, peaks. Names multiply around the male lead, and listeners spend these episodes asking each other who is who and whether the man they are hearing is the man they started with.

The audience here
The sharpest genuine story block in the run

Ep 291–310 sheds 2.11× the benchmark — the worst block that is not a paywall effect. Note that the retention cost is not caused by a bad plot turn: the plot here is good. It is caused by the audience being unable to track the protagonist's identity. Narrator complaints spike in the same window.

Act 6

The Kingdoms, and a Second Couple

Episodes 331–490
What happens in the story

The show opens out into a full fantasy epic: kingdoms, dark faes, bargains, a queen's trauma, an abyss beginning to stir. Gideon arrives around ep 390 and, with Vera, becomes a second romance running alongside Evie and Gavriel's.

For a long stretch the secondary couple carries as much of the screen as the leads do.

The audience here
The show recovers — and a secondary couple does not cost it

Blocks fall back to 0.88–1.31× for a hundred and sixty episodes. This is worth marking: across this batch a migration to a second couple is usually fatal, and here it is not. Evie appears in only about 39% of these episodes and the audience still holds. The difference is that the leads' story is paused, not replaced — and Evie comes back.

Act 7

The Abyss — and the Wall

Episodes 491–570
What happens in the story

The war reaches the abyss. Evie's visions, a barrier she must hold, an enemy that can track them to the ice; Vera and Gideon's own secret closes in. Evie is fully back at the centre.

Around ep 565 something outside the story happens: the free daily unlock stops, and listeners are asked to buy the remainder.

The audience here
A 6.75× block — but this one is the paywall

Ep 551–570 is the largest block in the run, and it is largely a monetization boundary, not a story break. Production and paywall complaints jump to 13% of comments here against 2–5% elsewhere, and the listeners name the mechanism precisely. Everything after this point is a paying remnant and is not comparable with what came before.

Act 8

Always and Forever

Episodes 571–665
What happens in the story

The last stretch is the war's end: dragons over the abyss, a council of kingdoms, grief, and a spell only Evie can break. She finally sees the difference in Gav's eyes, and has to let him go — flying against the wind and the darkness for one last sight of him before he is drawn back into Hell.

The finale, “Always and Forever”, is hers and Gavriel's.

The audience here
A paid remnant — and the leads keep it

Blocks run 1.9–3.7×, but this is the post-paywall audience and the comparison is not like-for-like. What matters creatively: this show does not hand its ending away. Evie appears in about 69% of the final hundred episodes and the finale belongs to the couple the audience came for — the opposite of the failure mode that ended several other shows in this batch.

Why it breaks — the mechanism

Not a bad turn — a tracking failure

Every other show in this batch broke because the story went somewhere the audience did not want to follow. This one is different, and the difference is worth internalising: the plot at the break is good. A buried past life for the hero is a strong reveal. What costs the audience is that they cannot track it.

  • Thirty episodes in the past. “Gavrael (Part I–XXVIII)” is roughly four hours of listening in which the present-day story does not advance. This is the same shape that cost Seduced by Darkness its worst block — the lead becomes a spectator in a backstory.
  • Two names, one letter apart. Gavriel and Gavrael are distinguishable on a page and nearly identical in the ear. On audio this is not a subtlety, it is a wall — the comments spend these episodes asking whether the name changed.
  • Narrator churn on top of it. Voice complaints cluster in exactly the same windows. When a listener is already unsure who is speaking, changing the voice removes the last handhold.

And then, honestly: the largest number on the chart is not a story problem at all. The 6.75× block at 551–570 is the free window closing. Production and paywall complaints run at 13% of comments there against 2–5% elsewhere, and listeners describe the mechanism exactly — that most of the show was free and the last hundred chapters had to be bought.

One honest counter-example, worth keeping. Between ep 331 and 490 this show hands a great deal of screen time to a second couple, Vera and Gideon — Evie appears in only about 39% of those episodes — and retention holds at 0.88–1.31×. Elsewhere in this batch a migration to a secondary couple was fatal. The difference is that the leads' story is paused, not replaced: Evie returns to the centre and the finale is hers. A second couple is not automatically a retention problem; losing the leads permanently is.

The one-line version: the audience came for Evie and Gavriel. For thirty episodes the show took them into the past and gave the hero a second name they could not tell from the first — and then, eighty episodes from the finale, it asked them to pay.
Part III · What to do
The stitch

Cut before the flashback — and keep one man, one name

1 · Cut at ~ep 230 (recommended)

Cut immediately before the thirty-episode flashback opens, with ~57% of the ep-51 audience still listening and every asset intact: the forced marriage, the devotion, the war, the dragons, and a heroine who has been driving since episode one. Continue with the past-life reveal delivered inside the present story — same information, no four-hour departure, and one name for the hero.

2 · Or cut at ~ep 290 (alternative)

If the flashback must be kept, cut after it and before the confusion peaks, at ~47%. Costlier: it inherits the sixty episodes the audience has already told us they could not follow, and it starts the new run with a hero the listeners are still trying to name.

This is a repair, not a rescue — the engine is not broken, and the largest number on the chart is a pricing decision. The three rules for the continuation: (1) One man, one name. Gavrael must not be one letter from Gavriel on audio — this is the cheapest fix in the batch and it addresses the most-cited complaint in the run. (2) Never leave the present for more than two or three episodes. Interleave any backstory against live beats; four straight hours in the past is what started the bleed. (3) Lock the narrator — above all during an identity reveal, where the voice is the only handhold the listener has. The test in the hour after the cut: can a listener say, out loud, who this is and when this is?
